The first hours after birth matter. At Penguin Hospital, every baby is received onto a newborn warming station in the labour room, checked straight away, and kept close to their mother. [CONFIRM: paediatrician present at birth, in-house or on call] We help with feeding, explain the first vaccines and screening, and tell you what to watch for at home.

- Skin-to-skin with the mother as soon as possible
- Breastfeeding started within the first hour, when possible
- Weight, length and head size measured
- Vitamin K given to prevent bleeding, with your consent

### Before you go home
- A full examination of your baby by a doctor [CONFIRM paediatrician]

### If your baby needs special care
Some babies need extra help with breathing, feeding, jaundice or warmth. [CONFIRM: NICU in-house, or transfer arrangement with a named hospital.] When a baby is expected to need intensive care, we plan the birth with this in mind.

**When to get help straight away.** Your baby has difficulty breathing or turns blue, has a fit, is very floppy or hard to wake, has a fever (or feels cold), stops feeding, has yellow skin in the first 24 hours or yellow palms and soles, or has blood in the stool or vomit. Call 108 or 112, or come straight in.

### FAQs
1. **Is jaundice in newborns normal?** Mild yellowing after the first day is common and usually fades within two weeks. Jaundice in the first 24 hours, spreading to the palms and soles, or a baby who is sleepy and feeding poorly needs a check the same day. A simple test measures the level.
2. **How often should my newborn feed?** Most newborns feed 8 to 12 times in 24 hours, about every 2 to 3 hours, including at night. Six or more wet nappies a day after the first week and steady weight gain are good signs that feeding is going well.
3. **When are the first vaccines due?** At birth, babies receive BCG, the first oral polio dose and hepatitis B, as per India's national schedule. The next vaccines are due at six weeks. Keep the vaccination card safe and bring it to every visit.

- **In pregnancy:** back pain, posture and preparing for labour
- **After birth:** pelvic floor exercises and safe return to exercise
- **After heart and lung surgery:** breathing exercises, walking plans and cardiac rehabilitation

### FAQs
1. **Do I need a special diet in pregnancy?** You need a balanced diet with enough protein, iron and calcium, not extra fat or sugar. 2. **When can I start pelvic floor exercises after birth?** Usually within a few days after a normal birth, gently. After a caesarean, start when comfortable. Regular pelvic floor exercises help prevent leaking urine and support recovery.

**H1: Signs of labour, and when to come to hospital**

**In brief:** Labour usually starts with regular, strengthening contractions, a "show" or your water breaking. For a first baby, come in when contractions are strong and about 5 minutes apart for an hour. Come straight away if your water breaks, you see bleeding, or your baby is moving less.

Most women worry about two things in the last weeks of pregnancy: will I know when labour has started, and will I come in too early or too late? Here is how to tell.

**False labour or real labour?** In the last weeks, many women feel practice tightenings (Braxton Hicks contractions). They are usually irregular, do not get stronger, and often settle when you rest, walk or change position. Real labour contractions come at regular intervals, get closer together, last longer, and become stronger whatever you do.

**The early signs.**
- *A show:* a small amount of sticky, pink or brownish mucus from the vagina. Labour may start in hours or days.
- *Backache or period-like cramps* that come and go.
- *Loose stools* or an unsettled stomach.
- *Your water breaking:* a gush or a steady trickle of clear or pinkish fluid.

**When to come in.**
- *First baby:* when contractions are strong, regular and about 5 minutes apart, each lasting about a minute, for at least an hour.
- *Second or later baby:* sooner, as labour is often faster. Many women come when contractions are about 10 minutes apart.
- *Always straight away* if your water breaks, if the fluid is green or brown, if there is bleeding, if your baby is moving less, if you have a bad headache or blurred vision, or if pains start before 37 weeks.
If you live far, in Wakad, Hinjawadi or Moshi, leave a little earlier, especially at peak traffic times.

**What to bring.** Your pregnancy file and reports, ID and insurance card, comfortable clothes, slippers, toiletries, maternity pads, a few sets of baby clothes, caps and socks, and a phone charger. Pack the bag by 36 weeks.

**Call us on the way.** Call +91 95189 04156 so the labour team knows you are coming. If labour is very fast, there is heavy bleeding, or you have a fit, call 108.

**FAQs**
1. *What if my water breaks but I have no pains?* Come in. Once the waters break, the baby is less protected from infection, and we need to check you and your baby. Note the time and the colour of the fluid.
2. *Should I eat in early labour?* Light snacks and fluids are usually fine in early labour at home. Once you are in hospital, the team will advise you, especially if an epidural or caesarean is possible.
3. *Can I have an epidural if I come in late?* An epidural takes time to set up, so ask early. If labour is very far along, there may not be time. Discuss your wishes at your antenatal visits.

**H1: Chest pain: when it might be your heart**

**In brief:** Heart-related chest pain often feels like pressure or tightness, not a sharp stab. It may spread to the arm, jaw or back and come with sweating, breathlessness or nausea. Pain lasting more than a few minutes is an emergency: call 108 or 112. Women and people with diabetes may have milder or unusual symptoms.

Chest pain has many causes: acidity, muscle strain, anxiety, lung problems and the heart. Most chest pain is not a heart attack. But the heart cannot be ruled out at home, and waiting can cost heart muscle. Here is what to look for.

**Pain that may be from the heart.**
- Pressure, tightness, heaviness or squeezing in the centre of the chest
- Spreading to the left or both arms, the neck, jaw, back or upper stomach
- With sweating, breathlessness, nausea, dizziness or a sense of doom
- Coming on with effort, stairs, a heavy meal or stress, and easing with rest (angina)
- Lasting more than a few minutes, or coming back

**Less typical signs, often missed.** Women, older people and people with diabetes may feel only breathlessness, unusual tiredness, indigestion-like discomfort, or pain in the back or jaw. Do not dismiss these, especially if they are new.

**What to do.**
1. Stop and sit or lie down.
2. Call 108 or 112. Do not drive yourself.
3. Take only the emergency medicine your doctor has already told you to take.
4. Unlock the door and keep your reports ready.
5. If the person collapses and is not breathing, start chest compressions.

**After the emergency.** If tests show narrowed heart arteries, the next steps may be medicines, angioplasty with a stent, or bypass surgery. Dr. Ashish reviews angiography reports with patients and families to explain which option makes sense and why.

**Lower your risk.** Stop smoking. Control blood pressure, sugar and cholesterol. Walk at least 30 minutes most days. Eat less salt, fried food and sweets. Get your numbers checked from age 30 if heart disease runs in your family.

**FAQs**
1. *Can young people have heart attacks?* Yes. In India heart attacks happen at younger ages than in many countries, sometimes in the thirties. Smoking, diabetes and family history raise the risk.
2. *How can I tell acidity from heart pain?* You often cannot, at home. Burning after meals that settles with antacids is more likely acidity, but heart pain can feel like indigestion. If in doubt, get an ECG the same day.
3. *Is sharp pain on breathing from the heart?* It is less likely, and may come from the lungs, the lining of the lung or chest muscles. Sudden sharp pain with breathlessness still needs urgent care, as it can be a lung clot or collapsed lung.
