**H1: Heart check-up (ECG, echo and stress test)**

A heart check-up looks at how well your heart is working, using simple, painless tests and a specialist's review. It is useful if you have symptoms, risk factors such as diabetes or high blood pressure, or a family history of heart disease. At Penguin Hospital, each check-up is designed after a consultation with Dr. Ashish Baviskar, based on your symptoms and health, so no test is repeated or wasted. [CONFIRM: which tests are done in-house]

### Who should consider a check-up
- Chest pain, pressure or discomfort, especially on exertion
- Breathlessness, or tiring more easily than before
- A racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat
- Fainting or dizziness
- Swelling of the feet
- Diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, smoking or kidney disease
- A parent or sibling who had heart disease young
- Before major surgery or starting a strenuous sport

### The tests
- **ECG (electrocardiogram).** Records the heart's electrical activity in a few minutes. Can show heart rhythm problems, strain and signs of a past heart attack.
- **2D echo (echocardiogram).** An ultrasound of the heart. Shows the heart's pumping strength, the valves and the size of the chambers. Takes about 20 minutes.
- **TMT (treadmill test, stress test).** An ECG recorded while you walk on a treadmill at increasing speed. Shows how the heart copes with exercise.
- **Blood tests.** Sugar, cholesterol, kidney function and others as needed.

### How to prepare
- Wear comfortable clothes and walking shoes for a TMT.
- Bring your old reports and your medicines list.
- Ask whether you should fast for blood tests, or stop any medicine before a TMT.

### After the check-up
Dr. Ashish goes through the results with you, explains what they mean in plain words, and tells you what to do next: lifestyle changes, medicines, more tests, a cardiologist's opinion, or, sometimes, surgery.

**When to get help straight away.** Chest pain lasting more than a few minutes, especially with sweating, nausea or pain in the arm, jaw or back, or sudden breathlessness or fainting. Call 108 or 112. Do not come for a routine check-up.

### FAQs
1. **Is a normal ECG enough to rule out heart disease?** No. An ECG at rest can be normal even when the heart's arteries are narrowed. If you have symptoms or risk factors, an echo or stress test may be needed. Dr. Ashish will decide which tests make sense for you.
2. **How often should I have a heart check-up?** If you are healthy and under 40, routine heart tests are not usually needed. From 40, or earlier with diabetes, high blood pressure, smoking or a family history, regular checks of blood pressure, sugar and cholesterol are wise, with heart tests as your doctor advises.
3. **Is the treadmill test safe?** For most people, yes. It is supervised, and the test stops if you have symptoms or the ECG shows changes. It is not done if you have certain heart conditions, which is why a doctor reviews you first.
4. **Do young people get heart attacks?** Yes, and more often in India than in many countries, sometimes in the thirties. Smoking, diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol and family history raise the risk. Chest pain should never be dismissed because of age.

**H1: Keyhole heart surgery (MICS)**

Minimally invasive cardiac surgery (MICS) means operating on the heart through a small cut, usually 5 to 8 cm, between the ribs on the side of the chest, instead of opening the breastbone. For suitable patients it can mean less pain, less blood loss, a smaller scar and a quicker return to daily life. Dr. Ashish Baviskar performs MICS and will tell you honestly whether it suits you.

### Operations that can be done by MICS
- Mitral valve repair or replacement
- Aortic valve replacement
- Some bypass operations (MICS CABG)
- Closing some holes in the heart in adults (ASD)
- Removing some heart tumours
[CONFIRM: operations Dr. Ashish performs by MICS]

### Who it may suit
It depends on your heart problem, your chest shape, your lungs, your weight and your other conditions. It is not suitable for everyone, and open surgery remains the safer choice for some operations and some patients. Dr. Ashish decides after reviewing your echo, angiography and CT scan.

### Compared with open surgery
| | MICS | Open (sternotomy) |
|---|---|---|
| Cut | 5 to 8 cm on the side of the chest | About 20 to 25 cm down the middle |
| Breastbone | Not cut | Cut and wired |
| Typical hospital stay | Often shorter | About 5 to 7 days |
| Return to driving and lifting | Often within weeks | Usually 6 to 8 weeks |

Both carry the risks of heart surgery, which Dr. Ashish explains before you consent.

### Recovery
- A short stay in the intensive care unit after surgery
- Walking with help the next day
- Breathing exercises to keep the lungs clear
- A gradual return to activity, with cardiac rehabilitation advice

### FAQs
1. **Is keyhole heart surgery less risky than open surgery?** It is not automatically safer. It mainly offers a faster, more comfortable recovery for suitable patients. The main risks of heart surgery are similar either way. The safest approach depends on your heart and health, and Dr. Ashish will recommend the one with the best balance for you.
2. **Will the heart-lung machine be used?** For most valve operations and hole repairs done by MICS, yes, connected through vessels in the groin or chest. Some bypass operations can be done on a beating heart without it. Dr. Ashish will explain what your operation involves.
3. **How soon can I go back to work after MICS?** Many people return to desk work in two to four weeks, earlier than after open surgery, because the breastbone is not cut. Heavy physical work takes longer. Your recovery plan will give you a timeline.
4. **Is MICS more expensive?** Costs depend on the operation, the hospital and the materials used. Our desk will give you an estimate before admission and help with insurance. [Compliance: no prices on the site]

**H1: Bypass surgery (CABG)**

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) creates new routes for blood to reach the heart muscle, going around narrowed or blocked coronary arteries. The new routes are made with blood vessels taken from your chest, arm or leg. Dr. Ashish Baviskar performs bypass surgery both on the heart-lung machine and on a beating heart, and discusses with you whether bypass, stenting or medicines is the right choice.

### When bypass surgery is advised
- Blockages in several heart arteries
- A severe blockage in the left main coronary artery
- Blockages with diabetes, where bypass often works better than stents
- A weak heart pump together with blockages
- When stents are not possible or have not worked
- Sometimes as an emergency after a heart attack

### Types
- **On-pump CABG:** the heart is stopped and the heart-lung machine takes over
- **Off-pump (beating heart) CABG:** surgery on the heart while it is still beating
- **Total arterial bypass:** using arteries instead of leg veins, for grafts that may last longer
- **MICS CABG:** for selected patients, through a small cut between the ribs

### What to expect
1. **Before.** Angiography, echo, blood tests and a talk with the surgeon and anaesthetist.
2. **The operation.** Usually 3 to 5 hours under general anaesthesia.
3. **ICU.** One to two days, with close monitoring.
4. **Ward and discharge.** Walking with help in the first days. Most patients go home in about a week.
5. **At home.** Wound care, medicines, a walking plan, and no heavy lifting or driving for about 6 to 8 weeks.

### Living well after bypass
A bypass treats the blockages but not the disease that caused them. Stopping smoking, controlling sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol, regular walking, and taking your medicines keep the grafts open for longer.

### FAQs
1. **Bypass or stent: which is better?** It depends on how many arteries are blocked, where, and on your other conditions, especially diabetes and heart pump strength. For blockages in several arteries, bypass often gives better long-term results. For a single blockage, a stent may be enough. A heart team, cardiologist and surgeon, should decide with you.
2. **How long does a bypass last?** Arterial grafts often stay open for 15 to 20 years or more. Vein grafts tend to last a little less. Not smoking and controlling cholesterol, sugar and blood pressure make a big difference to how long grafts last.
3. **When can I go back to normal life?** Most people walk around the house in the first week, return to desk work in 6 to 8 weeks, and resume most activities, including intimacy, by 2 to 3 months. Driving usually waits about 6 weeks, until the breastbone has healed.
4. **Is bypass surgery risky?** All heart surgery carries risks, including bleeding, infection, irregular heartbeat, stroke and kidney problems. For most planned bypass operations, the risk is low, but it is higher in older people and those with other illnesses. Dr. Ashish will explain your personal risk before you decide.

**H1: Heart valve repair and replacement**

The heart has four valves that keep blood moving in the right direction. A valve can become narrowed (stenosis) or leaky (regurgitation), straining the heart. In India, rheumatic heart disease, from childhood throat infections, is still a common cause. Dr. Ashish Baviskar repairs valves when possible, and replaces them when needed, by open or keyhole surgery.

### Symptoms of a valve problem
- Breathlessness, at first on exertion, later at rest or lying flat
- Tiredness
- Chest pain or pressure
- Dizziness or fainting
- Swelling of the feet
- Palpitations
Some valve problems cause no symptoms and are found because of a murmur.

### How it is diagnosed
An echo shows which valve is affected and how severely. Further tests may include a CT scan or angiography, especially before surgery.

### Treatment options
- **Monitoring** with regular echoes for mild problems
- **Medicines** to ease symptoms, though they cannot fix the valve
- **Valve repair**, especially for the mitral valve, keeping your own valve
- **Valve replacement** with a tissue (biological) valve or a mechanical valve
- **Balloon or catheter procedures** by a cardiologist for some patients

### Tissue or mechanical valve
| | Tissue valve | Mechanical valve |
|---|---|---|
| Lasts | About 10 to 15 years or more | Usually lifelong |
| Blood thinner | Usually only for a short time | Lifelong, with regular blood tests |
| Often chosen for | Older patients, women planning pregnancy (discussed case by case) | Younger patients |
Dr. Ashish will help you choose based on your age, lifestyle and wishes.

### FAQs
1. **Can a leaking valve be repaired instead of replaced?** Often, especially the mitral valve. Repair keeps your own valve, usually avoids lifelong blood thinners, and works well over the long term. Whether repair is possible depends on how the valve is damaged. Dr. Ashish will tell you after studying your echo.
2. **I have rheumatic heart disease. What should I know?** Rheumatic heart disease starts with untreated throat infections in childhood and damages the valves over years. Regular check-ups, preventive antibiotic treatment if advised, and timely surgery when a valve becomes severely affected help you live a full life. Treat sore throats in children promptly.
3. **Can I have a baby after valve replacement?** Many women do, but it needs planning, especially with a mechanical valve and blood thinners, which carry risks in pregnancy. Before surgery, tell Dr. Ashish if you hope to have children. Pregnancy is then planned jointly with an obstetrician, which we can do under one roof.
4. **When is the right time for valve surgery?** Usually when the valve problem is severe and causing symptoms, or when the heart starts to weaken or enlarge, even before symptoms. Waiting too long can let the heart muscle get damaged. Regular echoes help choose the right time.

**H1: Varicose veins**

Varicose veins are swollen, twisted veins, usually in the legs, caused by weak valves inside the veins that let blood pool. They are very common, especially in people who stand for long hours, after pregnancy, and with age. Many cause only cosmetic concern, but some lead to aching, swelling, skin damage and ulcers. Dr. Ashish Baviskar, a vascular surgeon, assesses them and explains the options, including laser treatment. [CONFIRM laser in-house]

### Symptoms
- Bulging, rope-like blue or purple veins
- Heavy, aching or tired legs, worse at the end of the day
- Swelling around the ankles
- Itching, cramps at night
- Darkening or hardening of the skin near the ankle
- An ulcer that does not heal

### Who is at risk
- Jobs with long standing: teachers, shop staff, traffic police, factory and hospital workers
- Pregnancy, and women in general
- Family history
- Older age, being overweight

### What happens at Penguin Hospital
- An examination standing and lying down
- A venous Doppler scan, an ultrasound of the veins, to find the faulty valves [CONFIRM in-house]
- A plan based on your symptoms and the scan

### Treatment options
- **Compression stockings**, leg elevation and walking
- **Laser or radiofrequency ablation:** a thin fibre inside the vein seals it from the inside, through a needle puncture, often as a day procedure
- **Sclerotherapy:** an injection to close small veins
- **Surgery:** removing or tying off veins, when other methods do not suit

**When to get help straight away.** Sudden pain, swelling and redness in one leg (possible clot), or bleeding from a varicose vein. Lie down, raise the leg and press firmly on bleeding. Call 108 or 112 if it does not stop.

### FAQs
1. **Are varicose veins dangerous?** Usually not, but they can worsen over time. Skin changes, ulcers, bleeding and clots in the veins are possible complications. If your legs ache, swell or your skin is changing colour near the ankle, it is worth seeing a vascular surgeon.
2. **Do stockings cure varicose veins?** No, but they help symptoms and can slow progression. They are useful in pregnancy, for long working days, and while you decide on treatment. Get the right size and pressure, and put them on in the morning before your legs swell.
3. **Is laser treatment painful?** It is usually done under local anaesthesia, through a needle puncture. Most people feel little during it, and mild tenderness or bruising for a week or two. Many return to normal activities in a day or two.
4. **Can varicose veins come back after treatment?** New varicose veins can form over the years, because the tendency remains. Regular walking, keeping a healthy weight, avoiding standing still for very long periods and wearing stockings at work help reduce the chance.

**H1: Leg artery disease (peripheral artery disease)**

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is narrowing or blockage of the arteries that carry blood to the legs, usually from the same fatty build-up that affects heart arteries. It is common in people with diabetes and in smokers. Without treatment it can lead to wounds that do not heal and, at worst, amputation. Dr. Ashish Baviskar, a vascular surgeon, treats PAD to protect your legs and your heart.

### Symptoms
- Cramping pain in the calf, thigh or buttock on walking, which eases with rest (claudication)
- Cold, pale or bluish feet
- Weak or absent pulse in the feet
- Hair loss and shiny skin on the legs
- Sores or wounds on the toes or feet that heal slowly or not at all
- Pain in the foot at night, eased by hanging it down

### Risk factors
Diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, kidney disease and age over 50.

### Diagnosis
- An examination of the pulses
- ABI (ankle-brachial index): comparing blood pressure at the ankle and arm
- Arterial Doppler ultrasound
- CT angiography to map the blockages before treatment

### Treatment
- **Lifestyle:** stopping smoking, a supervised walking programme, foot care
- **Medicines** to protect the arteries and the heart
- **Angioplasty and stenting** for some blockages
- **Bypass surgery** of the leg arteries, routing blood around the blockage
- **Wound care and minor surgery** for ulcers and gangrene

**When to get help straight away.** A leg or foot that suddenly becomes cold, pale, painful or numb. This is an emergency. Call 108 or 112.

### FAQs
1. **I have diabetes. How do I protect my feet?** Check your feet every day for cuts, blisters and colour changes. Never walk barefoot, wear well-fitting footwear, and keep sugar under control. Have your foot pulses checked at least yearly. Any wound that does not heal in a week needs a doctor's review.
2. **Is leg pain on walking just old age?** Not necessarily. Pain in the calf that comes on with walking and eases with rest is a classic sign of blocked leg arteries. It also signals a higher risk of heart attack and stroke, so it deserves a check-up.
3. **Will I lose my leg?** Most people with PAD never need an amputation, especially when it is found early. Stopping smoking, controlling diabetes, walking and timely treatment of blockages protect the legs. Wounds that do not heal need urgent attention.
4. **Can walking really help?** Yes. Regular walking, stopping when the pain comes and starting again, helps the body grow small new blood vessels around blockages. Many people can walk much further after a few months of a structured walking programme.

**H1: AV fistula for dialysis**

An arteriovenous (AV) fistula is a connection made between an artery and a vein, usually in the forearm, to create a strong, wide vein for haemodialysis. It is the safest long-term access for dialysis, with fewer infections and clots than a catheter. Dr. Ashish Baviskar, a vascular surgeon, creates fistulas and treats fistula problems.

### When to plan a fistula
A fistula needs about 6 to 12 weeks to mature before it can be used. So it is best made well before dialysis starts, when your kidney doctor says dialysis is likely in the coming months. Starting dialysis with a ready fistula avoids the risks of a neck catheter.

### Before surgery
- A vein mapping ultrasound to choose the best artery and vein
- Protect your non-dominant arm: avoid blood tests, drips and blood pressure cuffs on it

### The operation
- Usually under local anaesthesia or a nerve block, as a day procedure
- Takes about 1 to 2 hours
- A small cut at the wrist or elbow

### Caring for your fistula
- Feel for the "thrill" (a buzzing vibration) every day
- Exercise the hand by squeezing a soft ball, as advised
- Do not wear tight sleeves, watches or bangles on that arm
- No blood pressure checks, injections or drips on the fistula arm
- Keep it clean, and do not sleep on it

**When to get help straight away.** The thrill stops, the arm becomes very swollen, cold or painful, or there is bleeding that does not stop with firm pressure. Bleeding from a fistula can be heavy; press firmly and call 108 or 112.

### FAQs
1. **Why is a fistula better than a dialysis catheter?** A fistula uses your own vessels, so it has much lower rates of infection and clotting, and lasts longer, often years. A catheter in the neck is useful in an emergency, but carries a higher risk of serious infection for long-term use.
2. **Does fistula surgery hurt?** It is done under local or regional anaesthesia, so you should feel little during it. There may be some soreness for a few days. Most people go home the same day.
3. **What if my fistula does not mature?** Some fistulas need a small second procedure to help them grow, or a new fistula in another place. Regular checks in the weeks after surgery help spot problems early. A graft made of artificial material is an option when veins are not suitable.
4. **Can I use my arm normally?** Yes, for most daily activities. Avoid heavy lifting with that arm, tight clothing or jewellery, and pressure on the fistula. Never let anyone take blood or measure blood pressure on that arm.

**H1: Lung and chest surgery (thoracic surgery)**

Thoracic surgery treats conditions of the lungs, the lining around them (pleura), the chest wall and the space between the lungs. Dr. Ashish Baviskar, as a cardiothoracic surgeon, performs these operations and works with your chest physician to decide when surgery is needed.

### Conditions treated
- **Fluid or pus around the lung (pleural effusion, empyema):** often after pneumonia or tuberculosis
- **Collapsed lung (pneumothorax):** air leaking around the lung, sometimes repeatedly
- **Lung growths and nodules:** to diagnose or remove
- **Lung damage after tuberculosis:** some damaged areas need removal
- **Chest injuries:** rib fractures, bleeding into the chest
- **Growths in the middle of the chest (mediastinum)**
[CONFIRM: operations Dr. Ashish performs]

### Approaches
- **Chest drain:** a tube to remove air, fluid or pus
- **VATS (video-assisted thoracic surgery):** keyhole surgery through small cuts with a camera
- **Thoracotomy:** an open operation through a cut between the ribs, when needed

### Recovery
- A chest drain for a few days
- Breathing exercises and early walking to keep the lungs expanding
- Most patients go home in about 3 to 7 days, depending on the operation
- No smoking, ever: it is the biggest single step for lung healing

**When to get help straight away.** Sudden breathlessness or sharp chest pain, coughing up blood, or a chest injury with difficulty breathing. Call 108 or 112.

### FAQs
1. **My chest X-ray shows a spot on the lung. Is it cancer?** Not necessarily. Lung spots can be old infection scars, tuberculosis, benign growths or, sometimes, cancer. A CT scan and sometimes a biopsy help find out. Dr. Ashish will explain the findings and whether the spot needs watching, testing or removal.
2. **Why does fluid keep building up around my lung?** Common causes include tuberculosis, pneumonia, heart failure and, sometimes, cancer. Draining the fluid gives relief, and testing it helps find the cause. If it is thick or keeps coming back, keyhole surgery to clean the space may be needed.
3. **Will I be able to breathe normally after lung surgery?** Most people breathe comfortably after recovery, though removing part of a lung may reduce stamina a little. Breathing tests before surgery help predict this. Breathing exercises and walking after surgery make a big difference.
4. **Is keyhole lung surgery possible for me?** Many lung and pleural operations can be done by VATS, with less pain and quicker recovery. Some need an open approach for safety. Dr. Ashish will decide based on your scans and condition.
