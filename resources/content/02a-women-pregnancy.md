**H1: Pregnancy care (antenatal care)**

Antenatal care means the check-ups, scans and tests that keep you and your baby well through pregnancy. At Penguin Hospital, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande sees you from the first positive test to the birth, with scans, the labour room and the theatre in the same building.

### Who it is for
- Anyone with a positive pregnancy test
- Women planning a pregnancy who want a check-up first
- Women who moved to Pimpri-Chinchwad mid-pregnancy and need a new doctor
- Pregnant women who want a second opinion on their care

### What happens at Penguin Hospital
**First visit (ideally at 6 to 8 weeks).** We confirm the pregnancy, take your history, check your weight and blood pressure, and arrange your booking blood tests: blood group, haemoglobin, sugar, thyroid, and infection screening. Dr. Lakshmi explains which supplements to take and what to avoid.

**First trimester (up to 13 weeks).** A dating scan confirms your due date and checks how many babies there are. Around 11 to 14 weeks, the NT scan and screening blood test look at the risk of some chromosomal conditions.

**Second trimester (14 to 27 weeks).** Monthly visits check your blood pressure, weight, urine and the baby's growth. The anomaly scan, usually between 18 and 22 weeks, looks closely at the baby's organs. A sugar test around 24 to 28 weeks checks for diabetes in pregnancy.

**Third trimester (28 weeks to birth).** Visits every two weeks, then every week from 36 weeks. We check the baby's growth and position, talk about your birth plan, painless delivery and feeding, and explain the signs of labour.

Your own schedule may differ. If your pregnancy needs closer watching, Dr. Lakshmi will see you more often.

### How to prepare
- Note the first day of your last period.
- Bring previous pregnancy records, reports and any medicines you take.
- Write down your questions. No question is too small.
- Bring your husband or a family member to at least the first visit and the birth-plan visit.

### Looking after yourself
- Eat regular, home-cooked meals with dal, vegetables, fruit, milk or curd, and protein at every meal.
- Walk daily unless you have been told to rest.
- Avoid smoking, alcohol, raw or undercooked food, and medicines not prescribed in pregnancy.
- Sleep on your side in later pregnancy.

### After your visit
You leave each visit with your next date, the tests due, and any changes to your plan written in your pregnancy file.

**When to get help straight away.** Bleeding, leaking fluid, severe tummy pain, a bad headache with blurred vision, swelling of the face or hands, fever, or your baby moving less than usual. Call 108 or 112, or come to our emergency entrance. Do not wait for an appointment.

Fine print: Sex determination of the foetus is a crime under the PCPNDT Act. We do not disclose the sex of the baby.

### FAQs
1. **How often will I see the doctor during pregnancy?** For most healthy pregnancies, about once a month until 28 weeks, every two weeks until 36 weeks, and every week until birth. That is around 10 to 14 visits. If you have twins, diabetes, high blood pressure or another risk, Dr. Lakshmi will see you more often.
2. **Which scans will I need?** Usually a dating scan in early pregnancy, the NT scan around 11 to 14 weeks, and the anomaly scan around 18 to 22 weeks. A growth scan later is advised when needed. Many scans are done in our own ultrasound room during your visit.
3. **Can I continue working while pregnant?** Most women can work through pregnancy, with breaks, water and the chance to sit or walk. Heavy lifting, long standing, night shifts and exposure to chemicals may need changes. Tell Dr. Lakshmi about your work at the first visit so she can advise you.
4. **Is it safe to travel during pregnancy?** Travel is usually fine for healthy pregnancies, especially in the second trimester. Avoid long road journeys on bad roads, carry your pregnancy file, and check before booking flights late in pregnancy. Ask Dr. Lakshmi before any long trip, particularly after 32 weeks.
5. **What should I eat in pregnancy?** You do not need to eat for two. Aim for regular balanced meals with protein, vegetables, fruit, whole grains and dairy, and drink plenty of water. Avoid raw papaya in large amounts, unpasteurised milk and undercooked meat or eggs. Dr. Lakshmi will advise on iron, calcium and other supplements.
6. **When should I come in without an appointment?** Come straight in for bleeding, leaking fluid, a severe headache, blurred vision, sudden swelling, fever, severe tummy pain, or if your baby is moving less than usual. The labour and emergency team is here day and night.

**H1: High-risk pregnancy care**

A high-risk pregnancy is one where the mother or baby needs closer watching because of a health condition or a past experience. It does not mean something will go wrong. It means Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande builds a plan around your own risks, with more frequent visits and scans, and a team that is here day and night if you need us.

### Who it is for
A pregnancy may be called high-risk if you have:
- Twins or more
- High blood pressure, before or during pregnancy, or preeclampsia in a past pregnancy
- Diabetes, before or during pregnancy (gestational diabetes)
- Thyroid disease, heart disease, kidney disease, epilepsy or an autoimmune condition
- Two or more past miscarriages, a stillbirth or a premature birth
- A past caesarean, especially more than one
- Placenta previa (a low-lying placenta) or bleeding in pregnancy
- A baby that is growing too slowly or too quickly
- Age under 18 or over 35, or a pregnancy after IVF

### What happens at Penguin Hospital
- **A plan of your own.** At your first visit Dr. Lakshmi reviews your history and reports and writes down which visits, scans and tests you need, and when.
- **Closer watching.** More frequent blood pressure, sugar and growth checks. Dr. Lakshmi is trained in obstetric ultrasound and reviews many scans herself.
- **Working with other specialists.** If you have diabetes, a heart condition or a thyroid problem, we involve the right physician and share reports. [CONFIRM: visiting physicians]
- **Clear warning signs.** You go home knowing exactly what to watch for and when to come straight in.
- **A planned birth.** The timing and way of birth are discussed early, and changed if things change. The labour room and operation theatre are ready day and night.
- **Newborn readiness.** If your baby may need special care after birth, we plan for it in advance. [CONFIRM: NICU in-house or tie-up hospital]

### How to prepare
Bring every report from this and past pregnancies, discharge summaries, and a list of all medicines. If you test your sugar or blood pressure at home, bring your readings.

### After your visit
You leave with a written plan and our phone and WhatsApp number. Between visits, home readings can be shared on WhatsApp if Dr. Lakshmi asks for them. [CONFIRM]

**When to get help straight away.** Bleeding, leaking fluid, regular pains before 37 weeks, a bad headache, blurred vision or flashing lights, sudden swelling, pain under the ribs, fits, or your baby moving less than usual. Call 108 or 112, or come to our emergency entrance.

### FAQs
1. **Does a high-risk pregnancy mean I will need a caesarean?** Not necessarily. Many women with a high-risk pregnancy have a normal delivery. The way of birth depends on your condition, your baby's growth and position, and how labour progresses. Dr. Lakshmi discusses the options with you well before your due date, and again in labour.
2. **I have had two miscarriages. What can be done in my next pregnancy?** Before trying again, a visit to look for causes is useful. Tests may include blood tests and a scan of the womb. In the next pregnancy you will be seen early and often, with early scans for reassurance. Many women go on to have a healthy pregnancy.
3. **What is gestational diabetes?** It is high blood sugar that starts in pregnancy, found with a sugar test usually done between 24 and 28 weeks. Most women control it with diet and activity. Some need medicine. Good control lowers the risk of a very large baby and birth problems, and sugar usually returns to normal after birth.
4. **Can I have a normal delivery with twins?** Sometimes. It depends mainly on how the first baby is lying, whether the babies share a placenta, and your own health. Twins are often delivered a little earlier than single babies. Dr. Lakshmi will explain the safest plan for your twins as the pregnancy progresses.
5. **I am over 35. Is my pregnancy high-risk?** Age alone is one factor. Women over 35 have a slightly higher chance of blood pressure problems, diabetes and some chromosomal conditions, so screening and checks matter. Most women over 35 have healthy pregnancies. Dr. Lakshmi will tailor your visits to your overall health, not just your age.

**H1: Preeclampsia and high blood pressure in pregnancy**

Preeclampsia is a condition in pregnancy where blood pressure rises and organs such as the kidneys and liver come under strain, usually after 20 weeks. It can become serious quickly, so it is watched closely. At Penguin Hospital, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande checks your blood pressure at every visit and acts early when it starts to rise.

### Signs to know
Preeclampsia often has no symptoms at first, which is why every visit includes a blood pressure and urine check. Come in straight away if you have:
- A bad headache that does not go away
- Blurred vision, flashing lights or spots
- Pain under the ribs on the right, or in the upper tummy
- Sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet
- Feeling very unwell, vomiting, or breathlessness

### Who is at higher risk
- A first pregnancy, or a new partner
- Preeclampsia in a past pregnancy
- High blood pressure, kidney disease or diabetes before pregnancy
- Twins or more
- Age over 40, or a high BMI
- A gap of more than 10 years since the last pregnancy

If you are at higher risk, Dr. Lakshmi may advise a preventive medicine from early pregnancy. Do not start any medicine on your own.

### What happens at Penguin Hospital
- **Regular checks.** Blood pressure and urine at every visit, and blood tests for kidney and liver function when needed.
- **Home readings.** You may be asked to check your blood pressure at home and share the readings.
- **Treatment.** Medicines that are safe in pregnancy to bring blood pressure down, and closer monitoring of the baby with scans.
- **Admission when needed.** If preeclampsia becomes severe, you are admitted for close watch and treatment to protect you from fits.
- **Timing the birth.** The only cure for preeclampsia is the birth of the baby and placenta. Dr. Lakshmi weighs the risks to you and your baby to decide when, and how, the baby should be born.

### After birth
Blood pressure can stay high for a few days or weeks after birth, and preeclampsia can even start after delivery. You will have blood pressure checks before discharge and at follow-up. Women who had preeclampsia have a higher chance of heart disease later in life, so yearly blood pressure checks are wise.

**When to get help straight away.** Any of the signs above, fits, or your baby moving less than usual. Call 108 or 112, or come to our emergency entrance.

### FAQs
1. **Is high blood pressure in pregnancy always preeclampsia?** No. Some women have high blood pressure before pregnancy, and some develop it in pregnancy without organ strain (gestational hypertension). Preeclampsia is diagnosed when raised blood pressure comes with protein in the urine or signs that organs are affected. All three need closer watching.
2. **Can preeclampsia harm my baby?** It can reduce blood flow through the placenta, so the baby may grow more slowly. It sometimes means the baby needs to be born early. That is why growth scans and checks on the baby are part of the plan, and why acting early matters.
3. **Will I get preeclampsia again in my next pregnancy?** The chance is higher than average, but many women do not get it again. In your next pregnancy, Dr. Lakshmi will see you early, may advise preventive treatment, and will check your blood pressure closely from the start.
4. **Can I have a normal delivery with preeclampsia?** Often, yes. If you and your baby are stable, labour may be induced and a normal birth is possible. If the condition is severe or the baby is not coping, a caesarean may be safer. Dr. Lakshmi will explain the choice as it arises.
5. **What blood pressure reading is too high?** A reading of 140/90 or above in pregnancy needs a doctor's review. A reading of 160/110 or above, or any reading with headache, vision changes or pain under the ribs, needs urgent care the same day. If you check at home, write your readings down and bring them.

**H1: Normal delivery**

A normal delivery is a vaginal birth, where the baby is born through the birth canal. For most healthy pregnancies it is the safest way, with a quicker recovery. At Penguin Hospital, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande and the labour team support you through labour day and night, with pain relief if you want it.

### Who it is for
Most women whose pregnancy is healthy, whose baby is head down, and who have no medical reason for a caesarean. Many women who had one caesarean before can also try for a normal birth (VBAC) after a careful discussion.

### Preparing for labour
At your visits from about 32 weeks, Dr. Lakshmi talks with you about:
- The signs of labour and when to come in
- Pain relief choices, including an epidural
- Positions, movement and breathing in labour
- Who will be with you
- What happens to your baby straight after birth

### The three stages of labour
1. **Opening up.** Contractions slowly open the cervix (the neck of the womb). This is the longest part, often many hours for a first baby. You can walk, change positions, and have pain relief.
2. **Pushing and birth.** When the cervix is fully open, you push with the contractions and your baby is born.
3. **The placenta.** The placenta comes out within about half an hour after the baby.

### In our labour room
- The baby's heartbeat is monitored through labour.
- A newborn warming station is ready, so your baby is checked and kept warm straight after birth.
- Skin-to-skin contact and breastfeeding start as soon as possible.
- If labour slows or the baby shows signs of stress, the team can help with assisted birth or move to caesarean in our own theatre.

### When to come in
- Contractions that are regular, strong, and about 5 minutes apart for an hour (sooner for a second baby)
- Your water breaks
- Any bleeding
- Your baby is moving less
Call +91 95189 04156 on the way.

### After birth
Most mothers go home after about 24 to 48 hours. Stitches, if any, heal in a few weeks. See our postnatal care page for recovery and feeding.

### FAQs
1. **How long does labour last?** For a first baby, labour often takes 12 to 18 hours from the start of regular contractions, sometimes longer. Second and later babies usually come faster. Every labour is different, and the team checks your progress regularly so they can step in if it slows.
2. **Will I need stitches?** Some women have a small tear, or a small cut (episiotomy) to help the baby out, which is stitched afterwards with dissolving stitches. Many women need no stitches at all. Keeping the area clean and dry helps healing, which usually takes two to three weeks.
3. **Can I have a normal delivery after a caesarean?** Often, yes, if you had one previous caesarean with a low cut, the gap since then is enough, and this pregnancy is healthy. A normal birth after caesarean needs careful monitoring in a hospital with a theatre ready, which we have. Dr. Lakshmi will discuss the benefits and risks with you.
4. **Is it possible to have pain relief in a normal delivery?** Yes. An epidural is the most effective pain relief in labour and can be used in a normal delivery. See our painless delivery page. Breathing, movement, warm compresses and support from your companion also help.
5. **Can my husband or mother be with me?** [CONFIRM labour companion policy] A trusted companion can help you through labour. We will explain what is possible in the labour room at your birth-plan visit.

**H1: Painless delivery (epidural pain relief in labour)**

"Painless delivery" is the everyday name for a normal delivery with an epidural: a pain-relief medicine given through a thin tube in your lower back. It does not remove every sensation, but it takes away most of the pain of contractions while you stay awake and able to push. At Penguin Hospital it is given by an anaesthetist [CONFIRM: in-house or on call] and supervised by Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande.

### Who can have it
Most women in labour can, including first-time mothers and women having their second or third baby. It may not be suitable if you have:
- A bleeding or clotting problem, or take blood-thinning medicine
- An infection in the back, or a high fever
- Some spine problems or past spine surgery
- Very low blood pressure
The anaesthetist checks you before giving it.

### How it works
1. You sit up or lie on your side and curl your back.
2. The skin is numbed with a small injection.
3. A thin, soft tube is placed in the space near the nerves in your lower back. The needle is removed; only the tube stays.
4. Pain-relief medicine flows through the tube, and more can be given as labour goes on.
5. Relief starts in about 15 to 20 minutes.

### What it feels like
You will still feel pressure and tightening, and you can usually move your legs a little. Most women find the pain becomes mild or goes away. You can rest, and often sleep, through the long first stage.

### Things to know
- Your blood pressure and the baby's heartbeat are checked regularly.
- You may have a drip and, sometimes, a urine catheter.
- Labour's pushing stage can be a little longer.
- An epidural does not increase the chance of a caesarean.
- If you need a caesarean, the same tube can often be topped up for the operation, so you stay awake.

### FAQs
1. **Is painless delivery completely painless?** Not completely. Most women feel pressure and some tightening, especially when pushing, but the sharp pain of contractions is largely taken away. Sometimes relief is patchy on one side, and the anaesthetist can adjust the tube or the dose.
2. **When can I ask for an epidural?** Usually once you are in established labour, though there is no strict rule on how far along you must be. Ask early if you know you want one, because it takes time to set up. Very late in labour, there may not be time.
3. **Does an epidural harm the baby?** Only a very small amount of medicine reaches the baby. Epidurals have been used safely in labour for many decades. Your baby's heartbeat is monitored throughout, and the team watches your blood pressure, which can dip after the epidural starts.
4. **Will an epidural cause back pain later?** Long-term back pain after pregnancy is common, but studies show it is not caused by epidurals. You may have some tenderness where the tube was placed for a few days. Rarely, a headache can occur, which the anaesthetist can treat.
5. **Do I have to decide in advance?** No. You can say yes or no during labour. It helps to talk about it at your antenatal visits so you know what to expect. Your choice is respected either way.

**H1: Caesarean delivery (C-section)**

A caesarean is an operation to deliver the baby through a cut in the tummy and womb. Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande recommends one when it is the safer way for mother or baby, either planned in advance or as an emergency during labour. Our operation theatre is in the same building as the labour room and is ready day and night.

### When a caesarean may be advised
**Planned:**
- The baby is lying bottom first (breech) or sideways
- Placenta previa, where the placenta covers the cervix
- Two or more previous caesareans
- Some twin pregnancies
- Certain health conditions in the mother

**During labour (emergency):**
- The baby's heartbeat shows signs of stress
- Labour stops progressing
- Heavy bleeding
- The cord comes down before the baby

### What happens
1. **Before.** You meet the anaesthetist, sign a consent form, and stop eating as advised.
2. **Anaesthesia.** Usually a spinal or epidural, so you are awake but feel no pain from the chest down. General anaesthesia is used only when needed.
3. **The operation.** A cut is made low across the tummy, usually along the bikini line. The baby is born within about 10 minutes, and the whole operation takes about 45 to 60 minutes.
4. **Meeting your baby.** Your baby is checked on the newborn warming station and brought to you as soon as possible.

### Recovery
- You can sip water within hours, and walk with help the next day.
- Most mothers go home after 3 to 4 days.
- The wound heals over about 6 weeks. Avoid heavy lifting and driving until Dr. Lakshmi says it is fine.
- Breastfeeding can start soon after birth. Pillows and side-lying positions help.

### FAQs
1. **Can I choose a caesarean without a medical reason?** It is your right to ask and to be heard. Dr. Lakshmi will discuss your reasons and fears, and explain the benefits and risks of both ways of birth. For most healthy pregnancies, a normal birth is safer, with faster recovery, and she will support you to make an informed choice.
2. **How long before I can try for another baby?** Waiting at least 18 months between a caesarean and the next birth gives the scar time to heal fully. Use contraception in the meantime, and discuss your plans with Dr. Lakshmi at your postnatal check.
3. **Will I always need a caesarean in future pregnancies?** No. After one caesarean, many women can try for a normal birth next time. After two or more, a planned caesarean is usually advised. The decision depends on why you had the caesarean and how this pregnancy is going.
4. **When can I lift my older child or do housework?** Light activities such as walking and gentle housework can start in the first weeks. Avoid lifting anything heavier than your baby for about 6 weeks. If you have older children, sit down and let them climb onto your lap instead of lifting them.
5. **What should I watch for at home?** Come back if the wound becomes red, hot, swollen or starts leaking, if you get a fever, if bleeding becomes heavy, or if one leg becomes painful and swollen. Sudden breathlessness or chest pain needs a call to 108 or 112.

**H1: After birth: postnatal care**

Postnatal care looks after you in the weeks after your baby is born: your body's recovery, feeding, your mood, and planning ahead. At Penguin Hospital, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande sees you before discharge and again at about six weeks, and you can call us at any time in between.

### In hospital
- Checks on bleeding, blood pressure, temperature and stitches
- Help to start breastfeeding, including latching and positions
- Your baby's first checks [CONFIRM: paediatrician review, newborn screening and vaccines given before discharge]
- A discharge summary, your medicines and a follow-up date

### The first six weeks at home
- **Bleeding** (lochia) is heavy at first and gets lighter over 4 to 6 weeks.
- **Stitches** and caesarean wounds should be kept clean and dry.
- **Rest** when your baby sleeps. Accept help with cooking and housework.
- **Food and fluids.** Regular meals and plenty of water help recovery and milk supply. Traditional foods are fine; avoid very heavy ghee-laden diets that upset the stomach.
- **Movement.** Gentle walking from the first days. Pelvic floor exercises can start soon after a normal birth.

### Your six-week check
Dr. Lakshmi checks your blood pressure, healing, bleeding and mood, answers questions about sex and exercise, and talks about contraception.

### Mood after birth
Many mothers feel tearful in the first two weeks (the "baby blues"). If low mood, worry or loss of interest lasts more than two weeks, or you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, tell us straight away. Postnatal depression is common and treatable.

**When to get help straight away.** Heavy bleeding that soaks a pad within an hour, large clots, fever, a red or leaking wound, pain and swelling in one leg, breathlessness, chest pain, a bad headache, or thoughts of self-harm. Call 108 or 112, or come to our emergency entrance.

### FAQs
1. **When will my periods come back?** If you are not breastfeeding, periods may return in 4 to 8 weeks. If you are fully breastfeeding, they may not return for months. You can become pregnant before your first period, so use contraception if you do not want another pregnancy soon.
2. **What if breastfeeding hurts?** Some tenderness in the first days is common, but ongoing pain usually means the latch needs adjusting. Ask us before discharge or come back for help. Hard, red, painful areas in the breast with fever need a doctor's review the same day.
3. **When can I start exercising?** Gentle walking can start straight away. After a normal birth, most women can return to light exercise after the six-week check. After a caesarean, wait for Dr. Lakshmi's advice, usually at least 6 to 8 weeks before anything strenuous.
4. **Which contraception is safe while breastfeeding?** Several methods are safe, including condoms, a copper IUD (Copper-T) and progestogen-only options. Dr. Lakshmi will explain the choices at your six-week check, or before discharge if you prefer.
