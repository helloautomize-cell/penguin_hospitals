**H1: PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome), also called PMOS**

PCOS is a common hormone condition that affects how the ovaries work. It can cause irregular periods, acne, extra facial or body hair, weight gain and difficulty getting pregnant. In 2025, international experts proposed a new name, polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), because it affects metabolism as well as the ovaries. At Penguin Hospital, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande diagnoses PCOS carefully and builds a plan around your main concern.

### Common signs
- Periods that are irregular, far apart or missing
- Acne, or oily skin
- Extra hair on the face, chest or tummy, or thinning hair on the scalp
- Weight gain, especially around the waist
- Dark, velvety skin patches on the neck or underarms
- Difficulty getting pregnant

Not every woman has all of these. Some have only one or two.

### How PCOS is diagnosed
PCOS is diagnosed when at least two of these three are present, and other causes are ruled out:
1. Irregular or absent ovulation (irregular periods)
2. Signs of high male-type hormones, seen on the skin or in a blood test
3. Many small follicles on the ovaries on ultrasound

At Penguin Hospital this usually means a talk, an examination, blood tests (hormones, thyroid, sugar, cholesterol) and an ultrasound, often on the same day.

### How it is managed
There is no single cure, but the symptoms can be controlled well. Dr. Lakshmi focuses on what matters most to you:
- **Lifestyle.** Regular activity and balanced meals. Losing even 5 percent of body weight can bring periods back for many women.
- **Regular periods.** Hormone treatment or other medicines to protect the lining of the womb.
- **Skin and hair.** Treatments for acne and extra hair, with a dermatologist if needed.
- **Fertility.** Medicines to help you ovulate, ovulation tracking with scans, and referral for advanced treatment when needed. See fertility evaluation.
- **Long-term health.** Regular checks for diabetes, blood pressure and cholesterol.

### FAQs
1. **Can I get pregnant if I have PCOS?** Yes. Many women with PCOS conceive, some naturally and some with help to ovulate. If you have been trying for six months to a year without success, come for a fertility evaluation. Dr. Lakshmi will check ovulation, your partner's semen test and your tubes, and explain the options.
2. **Will PCOS go away on its own?** PCOS is a long-term condition, but symptoms often change with age and weight. Many women find periods become more regular after weight loss or as they get older. The higher risk of diabetes stays, so yearly sugar checks are wise.
3. **Do I have PCOS if my scan shows cysts?** Not necessarily. The "cysts" in PCOS are small follicles, and many women without PCOS have them on a scan. PCOS needs a pattern of symptoms and tests. Dr. Lakshmi will look at the whole picture before giving a diagnosis.
4. **Is PCOS the same as PMOS?** Yes. PMOS (polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome) is the new name proposed in 2025 for the same condition. It is meant to show that PCOS affects hormones and metabolism across the body. You may see either name on reports.
5. **What should I eat with PCOS?** There is no special PCOS diet. Regular meals with protein, vegetables, whole grains and pulses, and fewer sugary drinks, sweets and fried snacks, help with weight and sugar control. Daily activity matters as much as diet. Avoid extreme diets that are hard to keep up.

**H1: Irregular periods**

A period is called irregular when the gap between periods keeps changing a lot, is shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days, or when periods stop for three months or more. Irregular periods are common and often have a simple cause, but they can be a sign of a hormone problem. Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande finds the reason and explains what can help.

### Common causes
- **Puberty and the years before menopause**, when cycles are naturally less regular
- **PCOS**, a common hormone condition
- **Thyroid problems**, both underactive and overactive
- **Stress, illness, sudden weight change or intense exercise**
- **Breastfeeding**
- **Pregnancy**, always worth ruling out
- **Some contraceptives and medicines**
- **High prolactin**, a hormone from the brain

### When to see a doctor
- Periods stop for three months or more, and you are not pregnant
- Cycles shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days
- Bleeding between periods or after sex
- Very heavy or very painful periods
- Periods have not started by age 15
- You are trying to get pregnant

### What happens at Penguin Hospital
1. **A talk.** About your cycle pattern, weight, stress, medicines and plans for pregnancy. A period-tracking app or diary helps.
2. **Examination and scan.** An ultrasound looks at the womb and ovaries.
3. **Blood tests.** Usually thyroid, prolactin, other hormones and sometimes sugar.
4. **A plan.** Treatment depends on the cause: lifestyle changes, treating the thyroid, hormone treatment to regulate cycles, or fertility help if you want to conceive.

### FAQs
1. **Is it normal for periods to be irregular in teenagers?** Yes, for the first two or three years after periods start. Cycles can range from about 21 to 45 days. See a doctor if periods have not settled after three years, stop for three months, or are very heavy or painful.
2. **Can stress really delay my period?** Yes. Stress affects the part of the brain that controls the hormones of your cycle. Exams, work pressure, illness or a big life change can delay or stop a period. If it happens often, a check-up helps to rule out other causes.
3. **I missed a period but my pregnancy test is negative. What now?** Repeat the test after a week. If still negative and no period comes, see a doctor. Common reasons include stress, weight change, thyroid problems and PCOS. A scan and blood tests usually find the cause.
4. **Will irregular periods affect my chance of getting pregnant?** They can make it harder to know when you ovulate, and some causes, like PCOS, reduce how often you ovulate. Most causes are treatable. If you are trying to conceive and your periods are irregular, see a doctor early instead of waiting a year.

**H1: Heavy periods (heavy menstrual bleeding)**

Periods are heavy when they stop you doing your normal day: changing pads every one to two hours, passing large clots, bleeding through clothes at night, or bleeding for more than seven days. Heavy periods can cause tiredness and anaemia, and often have a treatable cause. At Penguin Hospital, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande finds the cause and explains every option, from medicines to keyhole surgery.

### Signs your periods are too heavy
- Changing a pad or tampon every 1 to 2 hours
- Clots larger than a one-rupee coin
- Using two pads at once, or waking at night to change
- Bleeding for more than 7 days
- Tiredness, dizziness or breathlessness

### Common causes
- **Fibroids**, non-cancerous growths in the womb
- **Polyps**, small growths in the lining of the womb
- **Adenomyosis**, where the lining grows into the muscle of the womb
- **Hormone imbalance**, common in teenagers and before menopause
- **Thyroid problems**
- **Bleeding disorders**, or blood-thinning medicines
- **Rarely, thickening or cancer of the womb lining**, especially after 45

### What happens at Penguin Hospital
- A talk and examination
- A blood count to check for anaemia, and thyroid tests
- An ultrasound of the womb and ovaries
- Sometimes a hysteroscopy (a thin camera into the womb) or a sample of the lining

### Treatment options
Dr. Lakshmi decides the right treatment for you after examining you. Options include:
- Medicines taken during the period to reduce bleeding
- Hormone treatment, including a hormone-releasing coil placed in the womb
- Iron to treat anaemia
- Hysteroscopic removal of polyps or small fibroids
- Laparoscopic removal of fibroids
- Hysterectomy, removal of the womb, only when other options have not worked and you do not want more children

**When to get help straight away.** Bleeding that soaks a pad every hour for several hours, fainting, chest pain or severe breathlessness. Call 108 or 112, or come to our emergency entrance.

### FAQs
1. **How do I know if my bleeding is really heavy?** If your period affects work, school, sleep or going out, or you feel tired and breathless, it is worth a check. Keep a note of how many pads you use each day and whether you pass clots. That helps Dr. Lakshmi understand your bleeding.
2. **Can heavy periods cause anaemia?** Yes. Losing a lot of blood every month can lower your iron and haemoglobin, causing tiredness, breathlessness, hair fall and pale skin. A simple blood test shows if you are anaemic. Iron treatment helps, but the cause of the bleeding also needs treating.
3. **Will I need a hysterectomy?** Most women do not. Many are helped by medicines, a hormone coil, or removal of a polyp or fibroid. Hysterectomy is considered only when other treatments have not worked and you have completed your family. The decision is always yours.
4. **My periods became heavy after 40. Should I worry?** Changes in bleeding are common before menopause, often because of hormone shifts or fibroids. But new heavy or irregular bleeding after 40 should be checked, sometimes with a sample of the womb lining, to rule out a more serious cause.

**H1: Uterine fibroids**

Fibroids are non-cancerous growths of muscle in the womb (uterus). They are very common, especially between 30 and 50, and many cause no problems at all. When they cause heavy periods, pain or pressure, or affect fertility, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande explains the options, from watching and waiting to keyhole removal.

### Symptoms
- Heavy or long periods
- Pain or pressure in the lower tummy or back
- Needing to pass urine often, or constipation
- A swollen tummy
- Pain during sex
- Difficulty getting pregnant, or miscarriage, depending on where the fibroid is

### Types (by where they grow)
- **Inside the lining (submucosal):** most likely to cause heavy bleeding and fertility problems
- **In the wall (intramural):** the most common type
- **On the outside (subserosal):** may press on the bladder or bowel

### What happens at Penguin Hospital
An ultrasound shows the number, size and position of fibroids, often on the day of your visit. A blood count checks for anaemia.

### Treatment options
- **Watch and wait.** Small fibroids without symptoms usually need only a scan every year or so.
- **Medicines** to reduce bleeding and pain, or to shrink fibroids for a time before surgery.
- **Hysteroscopic removal** of fibroids inside the lining, through the vagina, with no cuts on the tummy.
- **Laparoscopic myomectomy:** keyhole removal of fibroids, keeping the womb. Suitable for many women who want children.
- **Open myomectomy** for very large or many fibroids.
- **Hysterectomy** when fibroids keep causing problems and you do not want more children.

### FAQs
1. **Can fibroids turn into cancer?** Very rarely. Fibroids are benign. A rare cancer of the womb muscle exists, but it is not thought to develop from ordinary fibroids. A fibroid that grows quickly after menopause needs closer checks, and Dr. Lakshmi will advise you if anything on your scan needs further tests.
2. **Can I get pregnant with fibroids?** Many women with fibroids have healthy pregnancies. Fibroids inside the lining of the womb can make it harder to conceive or carry a pregnancy, and removing them may help. Fibroids can grow in pregnancy and cause pain. Dr. Lakshmi will advise whether yours need treatment first.
3. **Do fibroids go away on their own?** They do not usually disappear, but they often shrink after menopause, when hormone levels fall. If yours are small and cause no symptoms, regular scans may be all you need.
4. **How long is recovery after laparoscopic fibroid removal?** Most women go home in one to two days and return to light work in about two weeks. Avoid heavy lifting for four to six weeks. If you plan a pregnancy, Dr. Lakshmi will advise how long to wait, often three to six months.

**H1: Ovarian cysts**

An ovarian cyst is a fluid-filled sac on or in an ovary. Most are harmless "functional" cysts that come and go with your cycle and disappear on their own. Some need watching, and a few need removal. Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande, who is trained in gynaecological ultrasound and is a member of the International Ovarian Tumor Analysis (IOTA) group, looks at each cyst on the scan and explains what it means for you.

### Types of cysts
- **Functional cysts** (follicular and corpus luteum): the most common, usually gone within two to three cycles
- **Endometriomas** ("chocolate cysts"): linked to endometriosis
- **Dermoid cysts:** contain tissue such as hair or fat, usually removed
- **Cystadenomas:** fluid-filled growths that can become large
- **Rarely, cancerous cysts**, more likely after menopause

### Symptoms
Many cysts cause none and are found on a scan done for another reason. Others cause:
- A dull ache or heaviness on one side of the lower tummy
- Pain during sex
- Bloating or a feeling of fullness
- Changes in periods

### What happens at Penguin Hospital
- An ultrasound to look at the size and features of the cyst
- Blood tests, including a tumour marker when needed
- A plan: watch with a repeat scan, treat with medicines, or remove the cyst

### Treatment
- **Repeat scan** in 6 to 12 weeks for most simple cysts
- **Laparoscopic cystectomy:** keyhole removal of the cyst, keeping the ovary wherever possible
- **Removal of the ovary** only when needed

**When to get help straight away.** Sudden, severe pain on one side of the tummy, especially with vomiting, fever or fainting. A cyst can twist the ovary (torsion) or burst, which needs urgent surgery. Call 108 or 112, or come to our emergency entrance.

### FAQs
1. **Will my cyst go away on its own?** Most simple cysts in women who still have periods disappear within two to three months. Dr. Lakshmi usually repeats the scan to make sure. Cysts that stay, grow, look complex on the scan or cause pain may need removal.
2. **Can an ovarian cyst affect fertility?** Most cysts do not. Endometriomas, linked to endometriosis, and some large cysts can affect the ovary. When surgery is needed, keyhole removal that keeps as much healthy ovary as possible protects your chance of pregnancy.
3. **Is my cyst cancer?** The vast majority of cysts, especially in younger women, are not cancer. Features on ultrasound and some blood tests help tell the difference. Dr. Lakshmi uses an international scoring method (IOTA) to assess cysts and will explain clearly if further tests are needed.
4. **Can I exercise with an ovarian cyst?** Usually yes. With a large cyst, Dr. Lakshmi may advise avoiding very strenuous or twisting activity until it is treated, to lower the small risk of the ovary twisting. Sudden severe pain during or after exercise needs urgent care.

**H1: Endometriosis**

Endometriosis is a condition where tissue similar to the lining of the womb grows outside it, on the ovaries, the tubes, or the lining of the pelvis. It bleeds and becomes inflamed with each cycle, causing pain and sometimes scarring. It is common, often missed for years, and treatable. Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande takes period pain seriously and works with you on a plan for pain and fertility.

### Symptoms
- Period pain that stops you working, studying or sleeping, and that gets worse over time
- Pain during or after sex
- Pain when passing stools or urine, especially during periods
- Long-term pelvic pain
- Difficulty getting pregnant
- Tiredness

### How it is diagnosed
- A detailed talk about your pain
- An examination and ultrasound, which can show endometriomas ("chocolate cysts") and some deep endometriosis
- Laparoscopy, a keyhole look inside the tummy, which is the only way to see all types and can treat them at the same time

### Treatment
- **Pain relief** with medicines during periods
- **Hormone treatment** to reduce or stop periods and calm the endometriosis
- **Laparoscopic surgery** to remove endometriosis patches and cysts, and free up scarring
- **Fertility support** when pregnancy is the goal

Treatment depends on your pain, your age and whether you want to become pregnant soon. Endometriosis can come back, so follow-up matters.

### FAQs
1. **Is it normal for periods to be very painful?** Some cramping is normal. Pain that stops you going to work or school, does not settle with simple pain relief, or gets worse each year is not something to just live with. It can be a sign of endometriosis or adenomyosis, and it deserves a check-up.
2. **Can I get pregnant with endometriosis?** Many women with endometriosis conceive naturally. Some find it harder, because of scarring or effects on the ovaries. Surgery and fertility treatment can help. If you have endometriosis and have been trying for six months without success, see Dr. Lakshmi.
3. **Does endometriosis go away after pregnancy or menopause?** Symptoms often ease in pregnancy and after menopause, when hormone levels change, but pregnancy is not a cure. Dr. Lakshmi will never advise pregnancy as a treatment. Long-term hormone treatment or surgery may be needed to control symptoms.
4. **Will I need surgery?** Not always. Many women manage well with pain relief and hormone treatment. Surgery is advised when medicines do not help, when there are large cysts, or when fertility is affected. Keyhole surgery usually means a short hospital stay.

**H1: Menopause care**

Menopause is when periods stop for good, confirmed after 12 months without a period. In India it usually happens between 45 and 55. The years around it (perimenopause) bring changes that can affect sleep, mood, comfort and long-term health. Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande, a member of the Indian Menopause Society, helps you understand these changes and choose what helps.

### Common symptoms
- Hot flushes and night sweats
- Irregular periods, before they stop
- Trouble sleeping
- Mood changes, anxiety, poor concentration
- Vaginal dryness and discomfort during sex
- Needing to pass urine more often, or leaking urine
- Joint aches

### Long-term health after menopause
Lower oestrogen raises the risk of:
- **Thinning bones (osteoporosis)** and fractures
- **Heart disease**, which becomes the leading health risk for women after menopause
- **Weight gain around the waist** and higher sugar and cholesterol

That is why a menopause visit at Penguin Hospital also looks at your heart. When needed, Dr. Ashish Baviskar's heart check-up is in the same building.

### What happens at your visit
- A talk about your symptoms and how they affect your life
- Blood pressure, weight, and a breast and pelvic examination
- Tests when needed: blood count, sugar, cholesterol, thyroid, vitamin D, a bone density scan, Pap smear and mammogram referral
- A plan

### Treatment options
- **Lifestyle:** regular exercise, including weight-bearing exercise for bones, calcium and protein in meals, less caffeine and alcohol, a cool bedroom
- **Menopausal hormone therapy (MHT):** the most effective treatment for hot flushes, safe for many women when started under 60 or within 10 years of menopause, after a careful check
- **Non-hormone medicines** for flushes or mood
- **Vaginal treatments** for dryness
- **Bone protection** when bone density is low

**When to see us soon.** Any bleeding after menopause should be checked, even a single spot. It is usually not serious, but it must be examined.

### FAQs
1. **Is hormone therapy safe?** For many healthy women under 60, or within 10 years of their last period, the benefits of hormone therapy for symptoms and bones outweigh the risks. It is not right for everyone, for example after some cancers or blood clots. Dr. Lakshmi will check your history and explain the risks for you personally.
2. **I am 42 and my periods have become irregular. Is this menopause?** It may be perimenopause, the transition years before periods stop, which can start in the early forties. Other causes, such as thyroid problems, also need ruling out. If periods stop before 40, see a doctor, as early menopause needs specific care.
3. **What can I do about hot flushes without medicines?** Dress in layers, keep your room cool, avoid spicy food, caffeine and alcohol in the evening, and exercise regularly. Slow breathing during a flush helps some women. If flushes still disturb your sleep or work, talk to Dr. Lakshmi about treatment.
4. **Do I still need Pap smears after menopause?** Yes, until about 65, if your past results have been normal, following your doctor's advice. Screening for cervical cancer, breast cancer and bone health is part of good care after menopause.

**H1: Laparoscopic and hysteroscopic surgery**

Laparoscopic surgery (keyhole surgery) is done through small cuts in the tummy, using a thin camera and fine instruments. Hysteroscopic surgery is done inside the womb through the vagina, with no cuts at all. Both usually mean less pain, smaller scars and a faster return home than open surgery. At Penguin Hospital, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande performs these operations in our own theatre.

### Operations done by laparoscopy
- Removal of ovarian cysts, keeping the ovary
- Removal of fibroids (myomectomy)
- Treatment of endometriosis
- Hysterectomy (removal of the womb), when needed
- Checking the tubes and pelvis in fertility evaluation
- Ectopic pregnancy surgery
- Tubal sterilisation (family planning operation)
[CONFIRM each against Dr. Lakshmi's practice]

### Operations done by hysteroscopy
- Removing polyps and small fibroids inside the womb
- Taking a sample of the womb lining
- Dividing a septum (a wall inside the womb)
- Removing scar tissue
- Finding and removing a lost coil

### What to expect
1. **Before.** Blood tests, an ECG if needed, and a talk with the anaesthetist. You stop eating about 6 to 8 hours before.
2. **The operation.** Usually under general anaesthesia. Laparoscopy uses two to four small cuts, each about 5 to 10 mm. Hysteroscopy uses none.
3. **After.** Many hysteroscopy patients go home the same day. Most laparoscopy patients go home in one to two days.
4. **Recovery.** Light activity in a few days, most daily activities in one to two weeks, and full recovery in about four to six weeks after larger operations.

### Is keyhole surgery right for me?
It suits most women, but not every situation. Very large fibroids, a lot of scarring from past surgery, or some medical conditions may make open surgery safer. Dr. Lakshmi will explain why she recommends one approach over another.

### FAQs
1. **Is laparoscopic surgery safer than open surgery?** For many gynaecology operations, keyhole surgery means less pain, less blood loss, fewer wound infections and a quicker recovery. All surgery has risks, including bleeding, infection and injury to nearby organs. Dr. Lakshmi will explain the risks for your operation before you consent.
2. **How long will I be in hospital?** Most women go home the same day after hysteroscopy, and after one to two days after laparoscopy. After a laparoscopic hysterectomy, a stay of two to three days is common. You will be told the expected stay before admission.
3. **Will the scars show?** Laparoscopy cuts are small, usually one at the belly button and one to three low on the tummy, and they fade over months. Hysteroscopy leaves no visible scar at all.
4. **When can I go back to work?** After hysteroscopy, often in two or three days. After laparoscopy, usually in one to two weeks for desk work. Jobs with heavy lifting need four to six weeks. Your discharge plan will say when you can drive, exercise and resume intimacy.

**H1: Fertility evaluation**

If you have been trying for a baby for a year without success, or six months if you are over 35, a fertility check-up can find out why and what can help. The tests involve both partners. At Penguin Hospital, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande starts with simple tests, tracks ovulation with scans, and explains every option, including when to go to an IVF centre.

### When to come
- After 12 months of trying, if you are under 35
- After 6 months, if you are 35 or older
- Sooner if you have irregular periods, PCOS, endometriosis, past pelvic infection or surgery, two or more miscarriages, or if your husband has had a known problem

### The first tests
**For the woman**
- A talk about cycles, past pregnancies and health
- An ultrasound of the womb and ovaries
- Blood tests for hormones, thyroid and ovarian reserve (AMH)
- A test of the tubes (HSG or a saline scan), when needed

**For the man**
- A semen analysis, which is simple and often the most useful single test
- A physician or urologist review if needed

### What can help
- **Timing.** Knowing your fertile days.
- **Ovulation tracking.** Serial scans to see when the egg is released.
- **Medicines to help ovulation**, with scan monitoring.
- **IUI (intrauterine insemination).** Washed sperm is placed in the womb at the time of ovulation. [CONFIRM: IUI done in-house or consultation only]
- **Surgery** for fibroids, polyps, endometriosis or blocked tubes, when it helps.
- **Referral for IVF** when it is the better route, with your reports ready.

### FAQs
1. **Is infertility usually the woman's problem?** No. In about a third of couples the cause is in the woman, in about a third it is in the man, and in the rest it is in both or unexplained. That is why both partners are tested from the start. A semen test is quick and easy.
2. **What are my fertile days?** In a 28-day cycle, ovulation usually happens around day 14, and the fertile window is the five days before and the day of ovulation. Sex every two to three days through the cycle covers this window without stress. Cycles vary, so scans can pinpoint ovulation.
3. **Does PCOS mean I need IVF?** Usually not. Most women with PCOS who are not ovulating can be helped with weight management and medicines to trigger ovulation, with scan monitoring. IVF is considered if these do not work or there are other factors.
4. **How many IUI cycles should we try?** Many couples try three to four cycles of IUI if the tubes are open and the semen test is suitable. If it does not work, IVF may give a better chance. Dr. Lakshmi will review your situation after each cycle.

**H1: Pap smear, HPV test and colposcopy**

Cervical cancer is one of the most common cancers in Indian women, and one of the most preventable. A Pap smear or HPV test finds early changes in the cervix (the neck of the womb) years before cancer could develop. If a result is abnormal, a colposcopy looks closer. At Penguin Hospital, Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande offers screening, follow-up and advice on the HPV vaccine.

### Who should be screened
- Women from about 25 to 65, or from 30 with HPV testing, following your doctor's advice
- Every 3 years with a Pap smear, or every 5 years with an HPV test, if results are normal
- More often if you have had abnormal results

### The Pap smear
A short test in the clinic. A speculum gently opens the vagina and a soft brush takes cells from the cervix. It takes a few minutes and may feel uncomfortable but should not be painful. Avoid testing during your period.

### Colposcopy
If a result shows changed cells, a colposcopy uses a magnifying instrument to look at the cervix. A small sample (biopsy) may be taken. Treatment of changed cells, when needed, is a minor procedure.

### The HPV vaccine
The vaccine protects against the HPV types that cause most cervical cancers. It works best before any sexual contact, so it is advised for girls from 9 to 14, and can be given later. India's national HPV vaccination programme covers girls aged 9 to 14. [CONFIRM: vaccine given at Penguin Hospital]

### FAQs
1. **Does an abnormal Pap smear mean I have cancer?** No. Most abnormal results show minor cell changes, often caused by HPV, which frequently clear on their own. Some need a colposcopy and occasionally a small treatment. Screening exists to find these changes early, long before cancer could develop.
2. **I have had the HPV vaccine. Do I still need screening?** Yes. The vaccine protects against most, but not all, of the HPV types that cause cervical cancer. Regular screening is still advised for vaccinated women.
3. **Can I have a Pap smear if I am pregnant?** Yes, if it is due. It is safe in pregnancy. Dr. Lakshmi may postpone it to after birth if you were screened recently.
4. **Is colposcopy painful?** It feels like a Pap smear and takes a little longer. A biopsy may cause a brief pinch. Most women return to normal activities the same day. Avoid sex and tampons for a few days after a biopsy.

**H1: Medical termination of pregnancy (MTP)**

Ending a pregnancy is legal in India under the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, within set time limits and conditions, when done by a registered doctor at an approved centre. At Penguin Hospital it is handled privately and without judgement by Dr. Lakshmi Kalbande, with counselling before and care after. [CONFIRM: MTP centre approval]

### What the law allows (summary)
- Up to 20 weeks, on the opinion of one registered doctor
- From 20 to 24 weeks, for certain categories of women (such as survivors of sexual assault, minors, a change in marital status, or a serious foetal condition), on the opinion of two doctors
- Beyond 24 weeks, only for substantial foetal abnormalities, through a medical board
- A married or unmarried woman's own consent is enough if she is 18 or older. A guardian's consent is needed under 18
- Your identity is kept confidential by law

Sex-selective abortion is a crime under the PCPNDT Act. We do not provide it.

### What happens
1. **Consultation.** A private talk, a scan to confirm how many weeks and where the pregnancy is, and blood tests.
2. **Choosing a method.** Medical (tablets) in early pregnancy, or a short procedure (suction) under anaesthesia. Dr. Lakshmi explains which is suitable and safe for you.
3. **Aftercare.** A follow-up visit to make sure the pregnancy has ended completely, and advice on contraception.

**When to get help straight away.** Heavy bleeding soaking two pads an hour for two hours, fever, severe pain, or fainting. Call 108 or 112, or come to our emergency entrance.

### FAQs
1. **Is MTP confidential?** Yes. The MTP Act requires your name and details to be kept confidential. We do not share them with anyone without your permission, including your family.
2. **Will it affect my ability to have children later?** A safely done termination does not usually affect future fertility. Infection or complications from unsafe methods can. That is why termination should only be done by a registered doctor at an approved centre, never with tablets bought without supervision.
3. **Can I take abortion pills at home on my own?** Do not take them without a doctor's examination. A scan first confirms how many weeks you are and rules out an ectopic pregnancy, which pills cannot treat and which can be life-threatening. Medical termination is only safe under a doctor's supervision.
